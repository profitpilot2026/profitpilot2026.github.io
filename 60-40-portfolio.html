The Quiet Collapse of the
60/40 Portfolio — And What's Replacing It

For 40 years, 60% stocks and 40% bonds was the default. It stopped working in 2022. Most advisors still haven't updated the playbook.

5 min read May 11, 2026 Finance

The 60/40 portfolio had one job: when stocks go down, bonds go up, and the portfolio doesn't blow up. For most of the period between 1980 and 2020, it worked beautifully. The negative correlation between equities and Treasuries was so reliable that it became the foundation of mainstream financial advice. Then 2022 happened, and both stocks and bonds fell simultaneously — and the whole model broke.

Why 2022 was different, and what it revealed

The 60/40 portfolio's protective mechanism relied on a specific macro environment: when growth fears drove stock selloffs, the Fed would cut rates, bonds would rally, and your fixed income allocation would cushion the blow. This worked for four decades because inflation was generally low and the Fed's primary tool was rate cuts in response to slowdowns.

2022 was an inflationary shock. The Fed wasn't cutting — it was hiking aggressively. Bonds, which move inversely to rates, got crushed at the same time equities sold off. The traditional hedge provided zero protection at the exact moment it was supposed to kick in. A 60/40 portfolio lost roughly 16-17% in 2022 — the worst year for the strategy since 1937.

// The fundamental flaw

The 60/40 portfolio was never truly diversified. It was two assets that happened to move in opposite directions during one specific macroeconomic regime. The regime changed. The correlation changed with it.

What the updated playbook looks like

The honest answer is that no single replacement has emerged as the consensus successor. But a few additions are showing up consistently in more sophisticated portfolios:

Real assets: Infrastructure, real estate, commodities, and TIPS (Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities) tend to hold value in inflationary environments where both stocks and nominal bonds struggle. Adding 10-15% real assets allocation gives the portfolio something that performs in the macro scenario that broke 60/40.

Private credit: Floating-rate private credit actually benefits from rising rates, unlike fixed-rate bonds. It's less liquid, which is a genuine cost — but for investors with long time horizons, the illiquidity premium plus the rate sensitivity makes it a useful diversifier.

Managed futures / trend-following: These strategies have a genuinely different return profile from both stocks and bonds, and historically perform well in sustained trending markets — including inflationary ones. The 2022 period where 60/40 got destroyed was a great year for trend-following funds. That's the diversification that the bond allocation was supposed to provide.

"Real diversification means owning things that make money in different macroeconomic scenarios, not just different asset labels. Stocks and bonds are different labels. Real assets, trend-following, and private credit are different scenarios."

The advisor problem

Here's the uncomfortable part: most retail investors still have portfolios that look like a 60/40 variant because that's what their advisors recommend. The alternatives above require access to private markets, more complex fund structures, or the willingness to use instruments that most standard brokerage platforms don't make easy.

The retail investor is, once again, the last to get access to the tools that institutional investors have been using for years. Some of this gap is closing — interval funds, ETF wrappers for managed futures, accessible REIT structures — but it's closing slowly.

What you can actually do

For most investors, the practical answer isn't to rebuild the portfolio from scratch. It's to recognise that the bond allocation is doing something different now than it was in 2015. Adding a small TIPS allocation, a commodity ETF, and a managed futures ETF alongside a more traditional bond position is achievable through any standard brokerage account and gives you exposure to the macro scenarios that traditional fixed income now misses.

// The Bottom Line

The 60/40 portfolio isn't dead — it's regime-dependent. In low-inflation environments with a reactive Fed, it still works. In inflationary environments where the Fed is hiking, it fails. The question to ask your advisor: what happens to my portfolio if we get another 2022? If the answer is "the bonds will protect you" — they haven't updated the playbook.
