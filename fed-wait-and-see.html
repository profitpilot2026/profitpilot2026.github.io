Why the Fed's "Wait and See"
Approach Is Starting to Worry Markets

Patience is a virtue until it starts looking like paralysis. Here's what the bond market is actually pricing in right now.

3 min read May 13, 2026 Finance

The Federal Reserve has been saying "data dependent" so many times it's stopped sounding like a policy stance and started sounding like a stall tactic. Markets noticed. The bond market, specifically — which tends to have less patience for ambiguity than equity investors — has started pricing in something the Fed's language won't admit: that the central bank doesn't know what to do next, and is hoping the data makes the decision for them.

What "wait and see" actually means

When the Fed says it's being data dependent, the polite translation is: "We're watching inflation and employment and we'll respond accordingly." The less polite translation, given the current context, is: "We're caught between an inflation number that isn't quite dead yet and a jobs market that isn't quite weak enough to justify cuts, and we'd rather wait than be wrong."

That's not irrational. Being wrong on rate decisions has consequences that take 12-18 months to fully play out. Caution is defensible. But there's a cost to waiting too, and it's showing up in a few places.

// What the yield curve is saying

When short-term rates stay elevated while long-term rates flatten or fall, the bond market is effectively betting that the Fed will have to cut eventually — and that the longer they wait, the harder the eventual landing. That's been the shape of the curve for months now.

The credibility problem

Central bank credibility is built on the expectation that policy will respond predictably to conditions. When the Fed moves the goalposts — first it was inflation, then it was core inflation, then it was services inflation, now it's "the totality of the data" — markets start to lose the thread. If you can't predict how the Fed will respond to any given data point, you can't price risk accurately. And pricing risk inaccurately is expensive.

This is part of why equity volatility has been elevated even in the absence of a specific catalyst. It's not that markets think a recession is imminent. It's that the policy path is genuinely uncertain in a way it wasn't when rates were clearly going up or clearly coming down.

"The market can handle bad news. What it struggles with is not knowing which framework the Fed is currently using to define bad news."

What to actually watch

The two data points that will break the Fed's patience one way or the other: monthly CPI prints and initial jobless claims. If CPI comes in above 3% for two consecutive months, cuts are off the table for the year. If jobless claims spike above 250,000 weekly, the calculus flips and the pressure to cut becomes overwhelming regardless of where inflation sits.

Right now we're between those thresholds. Which is exactly why the Fed is doing what it's doing. The question is whether the economy stays between them long enough for the strategy to look wise in hindsight — or whether waiting too long turns a soft landing into something bumpier.

// The Bottom Line

The Fed's patience isn't irrational. But markets are starting to price in a scenario where patience becomes a mistake. Watch CPI and jobless claims over the next 60 days — they'll tell you more about where rates are headed than anything Powell says at a press conference.
