Europe's Defence Spending Surge:
Too Late, or Right on Time?

NATO members are finally hitting the 2% GDP target. But the gap between spending money and building capability is wider than the numbers suggest.

4 min read May 11, 2026 Global Politics

For most of the post-Cold War period, European NATO members treated the 2% of GDP defence spending target as a polite suggestion. In 2014, after Russia annexed Crimea, the target became more urgent. After February 2022, it became impossible to ignore. Now, for the first time in decades, most NATO members are either at or approaching the threshold. Headlines are calling it a transformation. The reality is more complicated.

Spending is up. Capability is lagging.

The gap between defence spending and actual military capability is large and largely underreported. Decades of underinvestment left European militaries with structural deficits that money can't fix overnight — and in some cases can't fix at all on any timeline that's relevant to current threat assessments.

The defence industrial base is the core problem. European arms manufacturers had spent twenty years scaling down production capacity to match reduced demand. Factories, supply chains, and skilled workforces were downsized. You can't rebuild that capacity in two years by writing larger procurement cheques. Lead times for major weapons systems — artillery, air defence, armoured vehicles — run five to ten years from contract to delivery under normal conditions.

Ukraine made this visible in a way that was uncomfortable for European governments. The continent struggled to sustain ammunition supply to a single active conflict zone without drawing down its own reserves. That's not a funding problem. That's a production capacity problem that predates the funding surge and won't be resolved by it for years.

// The political economy of rearmament

Hitting the 2% target also means different things in different countries. Some of that spending goes into genuine capability — new equipment, readiness, training. Some of it goes into defence bureaucracies, legacy systems maintenance, and politically connected domestic contracts that don't translate into frontline capability. The number is real. The composition varies enormously.

The interoperability problem

Even where European militaries are acquiring new capability, there's a deeper problem that spending doesn't address: interoperability. NATO effectiveness depends on allied forces being able to operate together seamlessly — shared communications systems, compatible ammunition, coordinated logistics, integrated command structures. Decades of independent national procurement decisions mean European NATO has a patchwork of incompatible systems that creates real friction in actual operations.

The push for more European defence integration — building a genuinely shared industrial base and common procurement standards — has stalled repeatedly on national sovereignty concerns and industrial politics. It remains stalled. Higher defence budgets spent on nationally distinct systems makes the interoperability problem worse, not better.

"Money is the easiest part of rearmament. Rebuilding industrial capacity, training pipelines, and interoperable doctrine takes a decade. Europe started in 2022. Do the maths."

The US factor

None of this analysis is complete without acknowledging the variable that's been driving European urgency more than any threat assessment: US reliability. The conversation within NATO about whether the American security guarantee remains unconditional has shifted in ways that would have been unthinkable a decade ago. European governments are not spending more because they've suddenly become more threat-aware. They're spending more because they're no longer certain they can outsource their security to Washington.

That's a structural change that will persist regardless of who is in the White House — because the domestic politics driving US ambivalence about NATO commitments didn't originate in any single administration and won't end with one.

Is it too late?

Not in an absolute sense. Europe has the industrial potential, the economic scale, and the political will — finally — to build genuine strategic autonomy in defence. But "too late for what?" is the right question. Too late to have equipment on the shelf in the next 18 months? Almost certainly. Too late to build meaningful deterrence capacity by 2030? Probably not, if the spending is sustained and the industrial investment is serious.

// The Bottom Line

Europe is spending more on defence. That's real and it matters. But the capability that money buys won't arrive on any timeline that matches current threat timelines. The more important question is whether Europe can sustain the political consensus to keep spending — through economic downturns, electoral shifts, and the fatigue that always follows urgency.
