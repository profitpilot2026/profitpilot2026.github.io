Why Every Tech Company Is
Suddenly Calling Itself an "AI Company"

Adding AI to your product doesn't make you an AI company. But it does make you more fundable. Here's the rebranding game playing out in real time.

3 min read May 11, 2026 Technology

Open any SaaS company's homepage right now and count how many seconds it takes before you see the word "AI." I'll wait. The answer is usually less than three. The pitch deck version of every B2B software company in 2026 starts with some variation of "AI-powered" or "AI-native" or, my personal favourite, "built for the AI era."

Most of them are lying. Not maliciously — but they're lying.

What actually happened

In 2023, it became clear that "AI" was the new magic word for fundraising. Valuations for companies with "AI" in their pitch were materially higher than those without, even controlling for everything else. Investors who had been cautious about SaaS multiples suddenly had FOMO. The market sent a very clear signal: call yourself an AI company and we will pay more for you.

The response was predictable. Companies that had been building perfectly good software for years added an AI feature — usually a chatbot wrapper over GPT-4, occasionally something more substantive — and updated their positioning. Their product hadn't fundamentally changed. Their website had.

// The 2026 tell

If a company describes itself as "AI-powered" on the homepage but the actual AI feature is buried three clicks deep in Settings → Beta → Enable AI Assistant — it's not an AI company. It's a software company with a feature.

Why this matters beyond the cynicism

The rebranding game isn't just annoying — it creates real signal distortion in the market. When every company is an AI company, the label stops meaning anything. Buyers can't distinguish genuine AI-native architectures from bolt-on features. Investors overpay for the former and underpay for the latter because they can't tell them apart in a pitch. And the companies doing genuinely interesting AI work get lumped in with the ones who added autocomplete to their search bar.

The distinction that matters isn't "does this product use AI" — almost everything will in five years. The distinction is whether AI is the product or AI is a feature. Those are fundamentally different business models with different moats, different defensibility, and different risk profiles.

"Adding a ChatGPT wrapper to your product makes you a software company with an AI feature. Building your product's core value proposition around a model makes you an AI company. Most companies in 2026 are the former pretending to be the latter."

How to tell the difference

Ask one question: if the AI stopped working tomorrow, does the product still work? If yes — if there's a usable product underneath — then AI is a feature. If the whole value proposition collapses without it, you're looking at an AI-native company. Neither is inherently better. But they're different things and should be evaluated differently.

The companies worth watching are the ones where the AI isn't a layer on top of an existing product but is woven into the data model, the workflow, and the output in a way that couldn't have been built five years ago. Those exist. There just aren't as many of them as the rebrand wave would have you believe.

// The Bottom Line

"AI company" is the new "cloud-based" — a term that was once meaningful and is now marketing shorthand. Stop reading the homepage. Start asking whether the AI would leave a hole in the product if you removed it. That's the only question that matters.
